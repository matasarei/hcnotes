> **TL;DR** — Every dark stretch in history ended because the people living
> through it built the way out, not because time passed. The world is shaped less
> by the powerful than by what the rest of us accept without reading — and today
> that includes answers from AI, pasted as-is. Better times are ahead. They just
> won't be easy, and they will be muddy, like every spring.

There's a line on my [About page](/about) that says I believe better times are
ahead. I wrote it a couple of years ago. Since then I've re-read it more than
once with the news open in the next tab, and each time it sounds a little more like
a joke I'm telling myself.

I'm keeping the line. Not out of stubbornness — because when I stop reading the
news and start reading history, the joke is on the news.

## Three cliches, and why I still buy them

You've heard all three. They get printed on posters and posted under bad days.

*Hard times make strong people, good times make weak people.* The funny part is
that this one isn't ancient wisdom at all — it's from a
[post-apocalyptic novel published in 2016](https://www.gmichaelhopf.com/hard-times),
and it went viral because it *sounded* old. That doesn't make it wrong. Everyone
I know who is genuinely hard to break got that way in a period they would never
volunteer to repeat. We are in one of those periods now. Whatever else it's
doing, it's forging.

*Even the darkest night ends and the morning comes.* This one is at least old:
a theologian [wrote it down in 1650](https://www.phrases.org.uk/meanings/darkest-hour-is-just-before-the-dawn.html),
and he was probably repeating something older. It's not a promise that the night
is short. It's a statement that night is not the permanent state of the sky, and
that somewhere out there the switch exists, whether or not you can see who's
holding it.

*Things have to go bad before they can go well again.* That's just the shape of a
cycle, and cycles are the one thing history is reliably full of. The question is
never *whether* there's a way out. The question is where in the cycle you are and
what you do while you're there.

## What the past actually says

I don't want to name anything from today here. Not a country, not a leader, not
a company. Partly because I don't want to make anyone angry, and partly because
it's beside the point: the pattern is older than all of them.

In the fourteenth century a plague killed somewhere between a third and half of
the people on the continent. It is hard to imagine a darker night. And yet the
survivors found themselves, for the first time in their lives, *scarce* — and
scarce things get paid. Wages doubled and tripled. The rulers of the day
[passed a law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351) freezing
pay at pre-plague levels, and it was ignored so thoroughly that it's mostly
remembered as an example of a law that didn't work. The worst thing that ever
happened to those people was followed by the best bargaining position their
class had ever had.

Five hundred years later, weavers looked at the new machines that made their
skills cheap and started smashing them. The state's answer was to make
[machine-breaking a hanging offence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_Stocking_Frames,_etc._Act_1812);
seventeen men were executed in one session. The looms stayed, and the mills
that grew up around them kept hiring for the rest of the century — just not at
hand looms, and not the same people.

Around 1900, [four in ten workers](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/8254/eib-3.pdf?v=75034)
in one large industrial country grew food. Today it is under two in a hundred,
and the country grows more food than ever. Nobody planned the other thirty-eight
into new jobs. The jobs came anyway, most of them in categories that didn't have
a name yet.

"Computer" used to be a [job title](https://www.nasa.gov/history/langleys-computers-1935-1970).
Rooms full of people doing arithmetic by hand, and doing it well enough to put
rockets into orbit. The machine took the name and the arithmetic. It didn't take
the people who understood what the numbers meant; it handed them better tools.

And the last century's worst stretch — two world wars with a depression between
them — was followed, within twenty years, by the richest and most peaceful
period the same places had ever known. Not because anyone waited it out. Because
the people who came through it rebuilt, and had learned exactly what not to
build again.

Every one of those dark stretches ended, and if you look at how, the same
detail shows up each time: somebody was building while it was still dark.

## The world does not change by itself

That's the part the posters leave out. "Better times are ahead" is not a weather
forecast, because times don't get better by themselves. People make them
better, or worse, and everything we have right now — every rule, every price,
every tool on your desk — is there because people chose it.

We like to think the choosing is done by the powerful few, and sure, they get the
biggest votes. But most of what shapes a society isn't decided by anyone
important. It's decided by the masses, quietly, through what they accept without
reading. The contract signed without opening it. The headline forwarded without
clicking it. The default kept because changing it takes a minute. Nobody
*decided* that; everyone just applied things as they came.

This is a blog about AI, so let me say where I see that same reflex today: in
answers pasted straight from a model into a place where they matter.

Lawyers have already been [fined for filing court briefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.)
that cited cases which never existed — the model made them up, and nobody
checked. A consulting firm [refunded part of a government contract](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2025/deloitte-to-refund-government-over-ai-errors.html)
after its report turned out to quote a judge on words the judge never said and
cite papers nobody had written. And that's just the cases that made it into the
news because someone downstream *did* read. Most of the unread output doesn't
make the news. It just becomes the codebase, the policy, the plan.

None of that is the model's fault, any more than the loom was at fault. The tool
did what it does. The failure was a human deciding that reading is optional.
I've [written before](/article/articles-code-got-cheap-the-plan-didnt) that code
you can't read is code you don't own. Scale that up. A society that applies
answers it doesn't read is a society that doesn't own its decisions — and then
it wonders who's making them.

The good news hides in the same sentence. If the world is shaped by what people
accept without thinking, then thinking is not a passive act. Reading the thing
before you apply it is the smallest possible vote, and it's the one that's
always available.

## The news is not the weather

About that next tab. Most media doesn't sell truth; it sells content, and bad
news is the content that sells best — measurably, down to
[each extra negative word in a headline](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4).
Bad things do happen all the time, the way weather does. That's not a reason to
watch every cloud, and it's definitely not a reason to read the forecasts piled
on top, which cost nothing to make and nothing to get wrong, because nobody ever
checks. Journalism is about as broken as the world it reports on -
the industry, not the people in it, who mostly do honest work under bad
incentives.

The odd part is that the same tool flooding the feed can drain it. A model that
reads a hundred sources in a minute will tell you what actually happened, what
is just guessing, and who said the same thing last year and was wrong. That's
better filtering and better forecasting than any pundit, and it sits on your
desk.

And after the filtering, you still have your own brain. Spend it on what this
means for you and what to do tomorrow, not on how to feel tonight. The news is
built for the second question, and the second question never built anything.

## Spring is muddy

I want to be careful with "better", because it's easy to hear it as "good".

It doesn't work like that. It has never worked like that. When winter ends, the
first thing that happens is not flowers — it's mud. Snow melts and the roads
turn into everything the snow was hiding, and for a few weeks it's honestly
uglier than the winter was. Nobody sane looks at the mud and concludes that
spring has been cancelled. The mud *is* the spring, just the early part. You
wash it off the roads, and you keep going.

That's the shape I expect. Not everything will get good. Some things will be
worse for a while in ways the winter made invisible. But the direction changes,
and the direction is what matters when you're deciding what to do with your
hands.

## The jobs will go. They always do.

AI will replace a lot of work. I'm not going to pretend otherwise; I'm using it
to replace my own, [every day](/article/articles-programming-is-fun-again), and
the part it replaces is real. That's exactly what automation did to weavers,
farmhands, and human computers. The pattern is not new.
What's new is that this time it's coming for the desk jobs, so this time the
people writing the eulogies are the people who own keyboards.

Two things the past says about it.

First: the count usually goes the other way. When cash machines arrived, the
obvious prediction was the end of the bank teller. Between 1980 and 2010 a few
hundred thousand machines went in — and the number of tellers
[went up](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm), from
about half a million to nearly six hundred thousand. Machines made a branch
cheaper to run, so banks opened more branches, and the teller who was left
stopped counting cash and started doing the parts of the job that needed a
person. The job didn't vanish; it changed shape, and the people who changed
shape with it were fine.

Second: complaining has a terrible track record. The weavers who smashed looms
were right about everything except the outcome. Their pain was real, their
wages did collapse, and the machines still won, because the machines were
better. The people who did well were not the ones who fought the loom. They
were the ones who learned what the loom couldn't do.

I already see the same story replaying at company scale: a firm announces a
model doing the work of hundreds of support agents, then a year later is
[hiring humans again](https://www.forbes.com/sites/quickerbettertech/2025/05/18/business-tech-news-klarna-reverses-on-ai-says-customers-like-talking-to-people/),
not for the routine tickets, which the model handles fine, but for the ones that
need judgement, patience, and someone to actually own the outcome. The volume
went to the machine and the responsibility came back to people, which is how it
has gone every time so far.

So: fit, don't complain. Not because complaining is beneath you, but because it
has never once worked, and fitting has worked every single time.

## How to get ready

The new times will not be easy. They will just be *different*, and different
rewards preparation more than it rewards strength. Here's what I'm doing about
it, roughly in the order I'd tell a younger version of myself.

Start with the cheapest one: read what you apply. Whatever the source — a model,
a headline, a contract, a diff — if you're going to act on it, read it first.
That's the whole lesson of every incident above, and it costs nothing but a few
minutes of attention. Make the machine explain itself until you could explain it
back, because if you can't, you're not using a tool, you're being used by one.

Then own your tools instead of renting the answers. Learn what the models are
actually good at and where they fall over, from your own hands, on your own
problems, not from someone's thread about it. The teller who understood the cash
machine kept the job; the one who saw it as an enemy, or as magic, didn't.

While you're at it, move toward the parts the machine can't hold. I've
[said before](/article/articles-programming-is-fun-again) that what stays with
the human is the thinking, the taste, and the refusal to accept a bad answer;
add responsibility to that list, and knowing *why* rather than just what. Every
wave of automation has paid those things more, not less, because they're what's
left when the routine is gone.

Build things, now, while it's cheap. The cost of trying an idea has never been
this low, and the people who come out of a hard period ahead are usually the
ones who spent it making something rather than refreshing feeds.

Keep a buffer — health first, then money, then skills, then people you can call.
Hard times punish anyone running with no margin. This is boring advice, and it's
boring because it has been right for every generation so far.

And don't outsource the thinking. The one thing you cannot delegate is being the
person who decides. That was true when the deciding was done by kings, and it's
true now that a model will happily decide for you if you let it. Don't let it.
That's the vote.

## The line stays

So the About page keeps its line. Better times are ahead — not because the
calendar guarantees it, but because every night on record has ended, and every
one of them was ended by people who got up in the dark and started building. This
is a hard time, and it is forging people. Some of them are reading this.

Bring boots. The roads are going to be muddy for a while, and that is exactly
what spring looks like.
